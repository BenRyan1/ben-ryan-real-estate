What's Actually Moving This Market

No boilerplate market-update language, just the mechanics: insurance, financing, and construction cost, and what they mean for your transaction in Santa Barbara and Montecito.

Insurance

What "Uninsurable" Actually Means for a Montecito Sale

There's a conversation happening in Montecito escrows right now that has nothing to do with price, and it's the one that actually kills deals. It's the insurance conversation, and if you're buying or selling above Highway 192, it deserves to happen on day one, not week six.

Here's the mechanism. Chubb, which for years covered roughly 60% of the Montecito market, has been steadily non-renewing policies on hillside and high-fire-severity addresses. That's not a Montecito-specific grudge, it's actuarial math: the 2018 Thomas Fire debris flow alone wiped out something like seventeen years of underwriting gains for this area in a single event. When an insurer's model says a zone is that exposed, the response isn't to raise the price, Proposition 103 limits how forward-looking that pricing can be, so the response is to leave.

What replaces Chubb is either the state's FAIR Plan, a bare-bones, fire-only policy that most buyers then have to pair with a separate wrap policy for everything else, or a surplus-lines carrier like Lloyd's, where I've seen documented cases of premiums running five to ten times the prior policy. Either way, a lender won't fund a loan without proof of adequate, insurable coverage. So a house that can't be insured, or can only be insured well below replacement cost, is a house that can't be conventionally financed. Full stop. That's a financing problem wearing an insurance costume, and it shows up in escrow, not before, unless someone checks first.

There's a real policy response underway. Insurance Commissioner Ricardo Lara's SB 824 would tie wildfire pricing to actual loss data, credit homes that have been hardened against fire, and stretch the non-renewal notice window from 45 to 225 days. That's meaningful, but it's not here yet, and it doesn't change what a buyer needs to know about a specific address today.

Which is why, on every listing I bring to market above 192 or on sloping terrain, I now document the current carrier, the renewal history, and the fire-hardening status, defensible space, roof class, ember-resistant vents, before the first showing. It's a small piece of paper that answers a question buyers are going to ask eventually anyway, and answering it early is the difference between a deal that closes on schedule and one that unravels forty-five days in.

If you're weighing a sale or a purchase above 192 this year, I'm glad to walk through what your specific address's insurance picture actually looks like, no obligation, just information worth having before you're deep into a transaction.

Financing

Why Cash Keeps Winning, Even Against Higher Offers

I've had more than one client ask some version of the same question this year: how did a lower offer beat mine? Almost always, the answer is the same. The winning offer was cash.

Nationally, roughly a third of home purchases in 2025 closed all-cash, a share not seen since the aftermath of the 2008 crash. Back then, cash meant investors buying distressed property at a discount. Today it means something different, fifteen years of home-price appreciation have left a large cohort of equity-rich buyers, move-down sellers, move-across relocators, and beneficiaries of intergenerational wealth transfers, who simply don't need a lender. In a market like ours, where the entry price already screens out most buyers, that cash share runs well above the national number.

The math is simple once you see it from a seller's side. A cash offer typically closes in ten to twenty-one days. A financed offer takes thirty to fifty, carries appraisal risk, and can still fall apart at underwriting in week five. Faced with two similar offers, a seller with any flexibility is going to take the one with fewer ways to fail, even at a modest price discount. That's not a market being irrational. It's a market correctly pricing certainty.

None of that means a financed buyer can't win. It means the bar for a financed offer has moved. The single biggest lever I've found is the difference between a pre-qualification letter and a genuinely underwritten pre-approval, one where a lender has actually reviewed income, assets, and credit and can commit to funding on a tight timeline. That letter doesn't erase the financing contingency, but it closes most of the gap that makes a seller nervous. I work with a small group of jumbo and portfolio lenders who can turn that around in 48 to 72 hours, and for any buyer who's serious about competing in this range, that's the first call to make, before you're touring properties, not after you've lost two offers.

The other lever is speed on your side of the process: knowing your number, having your documents ready, and not needing a week to decide. You can't out-cash a cash buyer, but you can close most of the distance between "financed" and "certain."

If you're gearing up to buy in this market and want to talk through how to structure a competitive financed offer, I'm happy to walk through it, and make an introduction to a lender who can move at the pace this market requires.

Construction Cost

Why "Just Build New" Isn't the Escape Valve It Used to Be

For most of the postwar era, when existing homes got expensive, new construction was the release valve. Builders responded to price signals, supply caught up, and prices settled. That mechanism is effectively broken right now, and understanding why matters for anyone weighing whether to wait for something newer instead of buying what's already on the market.

Start with materials. Tariffs on steel and aluminum have pushed those input costs up roughly 18% and 22% year over year, respectively, adding real money to every framed and finished square foot. Then labor: the construction industry is short somewhere around 350,000 workers nationally this year, and in trades like drywall and roofing, where immigrant workers make up well over 60% of the workforce, stepped-up enforcement activity has produced documented, sudden crew shortages, in one widely reported case, an 80-person roofing crew dropped to six almost overnight. The result has been wage inflation of 9 to 11% in specialized trades in high-demand regions, and construction timelines stretching from a historical six-to-eight months to nine-to-twelve.

Then there's the part specific to California and the South Coast: land and permitting. Permits, plan checks, and impact fees alone commonly run $15,000 to $80,000 per home here, CEQA compliance adds real cost and can add years of delay if a project draws a legal challenge, and the state's own figures put a mid-range new home, before you've bought the lot, at roughly $900,000 all-in. None of that is a temporary spike waiting to normalize. It's a structural cost floor.

The practical effect: the gap between what it costs to build a comparable new home and what an existing comparable home sells for has widened, and every signal points to it staying wide or widening further. That gap is what's actually supporting resale values right now, even in a market where buyer demand has cooled in places. "I'll just build" stops being a credible alternative once new construction runs meaningfully above what you'd pay for a well-located existing home.

That doesn't mean building isn't ever the right move, for a buyer at the very top of the market who isn't price-sensitive to build costs, custom construction is still a real option. But for the vast majority of buyers in our market, well-located existing inventory is doing double duty right now: it's both the more available option and, increasingly, the more rational one.

If you're weighing buy-versus-build on a specific property or lot, I'm glad to help you actually run those numbers side by side.
